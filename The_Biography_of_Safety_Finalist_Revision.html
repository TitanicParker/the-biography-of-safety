I / SEVEN MOVEMENTS

The eighth day

On Monday 10 July 2017, a man stood in a Galway hospital cubicle and demanded to know why he was being discharged while the problem in his feet remained unexplained. He had spent a week inside neurology. He had been examined, scanned, discussed, medicated. He had also been given a new language for his condition. But the question that had brought him from his general practitioner into hospital—the relationship between the painful, misshapen feet and the profoundly abnormal movement of his body—had not been settled.

He compared it to sending a car through its full roadworthiness test and returning it on bald tyres. It was an objection to the completeness of an investigation, not a demand for one particular drug or diagnosis. The exchange is the patient’s direct testimony. The surviving clinical notes do not document it. That absence will become part of this story.

“Are you going to bring me in here, give me a full NCT and send me out on bald tyres?”

The exact wording is preserved here as the patient's recollection, not as an institutional transcript. The neurologist's response, as he remembers it, was that arranging a podiatry appointment was as easy for the patient as for the doctor. To the patient this was no answer. Podiatry had been part of his life for years. He knew its practical value. He was asking why the specialist examination had not satisfactorily connected the feet to the motor illness being investigated.

What makes the timing consequential is not that the patient knew the diagnosis or could prescribe the right test. He did not ask for levodopa or announce that he had dystonia. He identified an unfinished part of the assessment while discharge remained reversible. A clinician could have examined further, documented the concern, explained the limits of investigation, commissioned specific follow-up, or assigned responsibility for investigating the foot–gait relationship. There was no requirement that all uncertainty disappear before leaving hospital. But the disagreement about the adequacy of the inquiry was itself new information.

The eighth day was an opportunity for immediate care, but also for organisational learning. Someone could have asked how the GP's reference to toe-clawing disappeared from the shortened admission summary, and whether this was a one-off oversight or a vulnerability of a wider system. That opportunity did not require knowing the patient's eventual genetic result. It required noticing that a patient had identified a discrepancy between what he came for and what the hospital believed it had completed.

Long before the hospital, the pain was measured in shoes. The outside of his left foot had its own geography, close to the fifth toe, where pressure gathered and a corn could make weight-bearing punishing. He tried insoles and plasters; he learned what footwear he could tolerate and how long he could remain standing. These were not incidental complaints made between neurological appointments. They were among the conditions under which he lived his ordinary life.

For years he worked in Galway bars, standing through shifts, moving stock, tending to customers, negotiating the small urgencies of a crowded room. He played darts. To throw, walk to the board, retrieve, turn and begin again once seemed too ordinary to notice; then each part of the sequence required adjustment. A person does not experience the loss of a livelihood or a pastime as a change in a diagnostic category. He experiences the missing hours, the shoes he cannot wear, the effort required to cross a room. By the time neurology admitted him, pain had already taught him to arrange his days around his feet.

II / SEVEN MOVEMENTS

The meaning of a symptom

To understand the question left behind in that cubicle, begin eleven days before admission, when it was still visible on the page.

On 29 June 2017, the general practitioner wrote to the hospital about a man of thirty-nine whose condition was deteriorating. The GP had first observed an unusual stiff, festinating gait when the patient attended with bilateral foot problems. As the weeks passed, the picture widened. Movement was sometimes hard to initiate. All four limbs seemed increasingly stiff. Tone was increased; reflexes were brisk. Dexterity and ordinary work were becoming difficult. There was a possibility of parkinsonism, but the GP did not pretend to know the answer.

GP referral · 29 June 2017

“He describes feeling that he cannot relax his muscles and that this is most problematic in bed — that he claws his toes and cannot get comfortable.”

Clinical referrals are acts of compression. A GP knows a patient through several appointments and must persuade a busy receiving service to see a pattern in a short letter. The urgent hospital then compresses again: triage, summary, handover, admission list, examination. Some compression is necessary. Hospitals cannot reproduce every consultation at full length. But there is a distinction between shortening a history and changing the clinical object that is handed onward. A description of gait, tone and painful toe-clawing asks one question. A description of tone and gait without the toes may ask another.

In the hospital's shortened representation, the difficulty initiating movement, stiffness in all four limbs and wrist cogwheeling survived. The bilateral foot problems and toe-clawing did not. This did not prevent a neurologist from later recording foot abnormalities; it did change what was foregrounded at the point where the problem entered specialist care. The loss was not necessarily of every fact. It was potentially of the relationship among facts: foot pain, toe posture, muscle tension, gait and function, presented as one evolving clinical puzzle.

The neurologist did not fail to recognise serious disease. That is precisely why the case resists an easy story. The admission beginning on 3 July 2017 produced substantial neurological findings: striking rigidity, gross slowness of movement, impaired initiation, an extraordinary gait, and a pattern sufficiently unusual to prompt consideration of young-onset hereditary parkinsonism. The clinician noted relatively little tremor and raised genetic possibilities, including PARK2, now commonly called PRKN. This was not an empty consultation.

Neurological examination · July 2017

“The gait is strikingly abnormal.”

That second observation is sophisticated. A leg or foot may seem to drop because of weakness, but abnormal involuntary posturing can imitate the appearance. The neurologist distinguished the look of a movement from its possible mechanism. He also recorded pes planus and hammertoes. No fair account can say that the feet were entirely invisible. They were seen, described and directed toward podiatric care.

Yet visibility is not integration. A neurologist may observe dystonic-looking gait and structural abnormalities without settling whether abnormal posturing contributes to pressure lesions, whether the toes curl dynamically, whether standing or walking changes the distribution of pain, or whether separate causes coexist and interact. A podiatrist may competently treat a corn without determining why a particular foot is repeatedly overloaded. Both services can perform their proper tasks while the relationship between those tasks remains unowned.

Somewhere in the first days of the admission, the neurologist wrote down a conversation about the patient's earlier life. There had been years of tension, perhaps beginning around 2004; a 2014 hospital encounter for bodily tension that had not resulted in neurological specialist review; a long history of corns, calluses and fallen arches. After leaving bar work in 2012, the patient had spent less time standing. The pressure pain in his feet diminished, and he became more aware of the leg tension that remained.

He was describing different experiences in the same body. His feet could hurt when bearing weight, while his legs could feel tense independently of that pain. Anyone who has experienced two sensations can distinguish them without knowing whether one condition contributes to both. The first record was close to that ordinary human distinction.

Three documentary states · July 2017

Typed history: he “began to realise” they were “not the same problem.”

Consultant impression: he had “markedly divorced” the feelings of leg tension from corns, calluses and foot pain “in his own mind.”

Between handwriting and formal impression, the patient's account gained an authority he had not claimed. He could distinguish pain from tension. The typed account made them different problems. The clinical impression presented that separation as something the patient had 'markedly divorced' in his own mind. The journey of these words matters because the writer was also the specialist deciding what belonged together.

We do not know every word spoken during the interview; the earliest surviving written note is the doctor's handwriting, not a recording. A later typed account could also reflect further discussion. Those limits matter. They do not erase the textual direction of travel. Each surviving version gives the separation a little more force. By the final impression, the patient can appear partly responsible for authorising a division of symptoms whose causes he was in no position to diagnose.

III / SEVEN MOVEMENTS

The frame that survived

In the final impression, the consultant proposed that the residual leg and foot symptoms, including burning, corns and calluses, might have been completely separate, or perhaps reflected a secondary peripheral neuropathy, possibly related in some way to historical alcohol intake. The passage ended, properly enough, with the admission that the matter was not clear. A clinician is entitled to uncertainty. The trouble begins when uncertainty has practical effects that are not themselves revisited.

Discharge synthesis · 2017

“May have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy … Not clear.”

“Plan/Recommendations: As per above.”

Yet a policy of waiting needs a way to know when waiting should end. What level of disability, pain, gait deterioration, medication failure or specialist advice would reopen the choice? And a plan dividing podiatric from neurological management needs someone to reconsider whether the boundary itself remains justified. The text provides an extensive rationale for caution, but the accessible record does not expose an equally clear rule for reassembling the original foot-centred problem.

It is important not to mistake the discharge document for a photograph taken on 11 July. The completed specialist account incorporated later material, including the DaTscan of 13 July showing markedly diminished tracer uptake in the basal ganglia. It is a composite product of several moments. We cannot place every phrase inside the neurologist's mind on the day of the protest. What we can examine is the finished document's effect: a plausible, elaborate account of young-onset parkinsonism, an uncertain compartment for the painful feet, and an operative plan through which care would continue.

At that point the record was installed as an authority. It did not need eight years to become important. It came from the consultant, returned to the referring GP, and could orient specialist nurses, prescriptions, referrals and further clinic letters. The question left unresolved at discharge was not neutral once embedded in that architecture. It was a question with an assigned home and a growing history.

There is a particular poignancy to the institutional surroundings. The neurology department in Galway was not isolated from the emerging genetics of young-onset Parkinson's disease. Professor Timothy Counihan, associated with the patient's early treatment deliberations, had co-authored research with Professor Daniel Healy on Parkin disease years earlier. In August 2017, shortly after this admission, Galway-associated clinicians published a clinical reasoning article about dystonic foot movement mimicking foot drop and genetically confirmed PRKN disease. The overlap of subject matter—young onset, dystonic foot appearance, genetic mechanism, treatment caution—is striking.

Publication is not proof of bedside availability. A scholarly paper does not tell us which clinician was on the ward on a particular morning, whether the authors were asked for advice, or whether another case's treatment was suitable for this patient. It does show that these questions existed within the professional environment surrounding the admission. They were not unknown problems waiting to be invented by medicine in some distant future.

The question was whether that knowledge would find its way into this patient's care as a relationship to be tested. A department can possess the relevant expertise and still fail to bring it to bear at the necessary moment.

By October 2017, procyclidine—the anticholinergic drug known as Kemadrin—had produced changes the patient experienced as extraordinary. His walk came back together. Legs felt loose and limber. Writing and ordinary dexterity improved. He could stand straighter and move with an ease he had feared lost. The record contains contemporaneous descriptions of these improvements; one expression of wonder was that it felt like living in a miracle.

Then came an interruption in treatment. Tension returned, the feet became restless, and the walk began falling apart. In later recollection, the patient's familiar focal left-foot pain was the first warning. The precise first-symptom order is a retrospective report; the broader movement of feet, tension and gait with medication state was reported close to the time. This matters because the body was generating information about the original division.

There was no need to abandon podiatry. There was a need to ask whether the neurological treatment response had new interpretive force. The patient was not demanding one elegant cause for every symptom. The history made a simpler demand: if the gait, leg tension and feet change together, what happens to the original assertion that the painful feet might belong to a separate compartment?

The patient did not pass invisibly through a vast and anonymous machine. The same general practitioner remained involved. The treating neurologist remained involved. Specialist nursing joined the network. Foot care continued. There were appointments, correspondence, telephone calls, prescribing decisions and genuine attempts to manage an unusually difficult disorder. A claim that nothing was done would misrepresent the record. A claim that nobody ever reconsidered anything would also be wrong.

There was continuity of personnel and correspondence, but the later records do not show a comparably sustained reconsideration of the original foot–gait relationship.

In 2020 the neurologist recognised that an apparent foot-drop movement was Parkinsonian rather than weakness. In 2021 procyclidine became difficult to continue because of raised intraocular pressure, and levodopa entered treatment. Later that year, altered gait and re-emerging dystonia prompted further changes. These were not trivial findings. Yet their appearance in the treatment record did not produce an identifiable reconstruction of what the painful feet had meant at the original admission.

The most precise documentary mechanism in this case is not simply the strengthening of the patient's early comment. It is the changing status of a clinician-generated hypothesis. In 2017 the formulation asked, in effect: could these foot symptoms be peripheral neuropathy? It said perhaps, possibly, not clear. Years later the continuing language could speak of neuropathic pain while uncertainty attached instead to what caused it.

The difference is one question mark. In 2017, the note left open whether the symptoms were neuropathic at all. In 2025, the clinician described 'neuropathic pain (of whatever cause)'. The uncertainty had moved downstream: not *whether* the classification held, but *what caused* the condition already named. A reader moving quickly through the records might never notice that shift.

Pregabalin deserves particular attention. Its use is real clinical activity, not proof of an underlying peripheral neuropathy. Burning pain can justify considering neuropathic mechanisms without independently establishing damaged peripheral nerves as their source. Alcohol exposure may properly belong in a differential diagnosis without proving that alcohol caused this person's feet to hurt. Structural lesions can generate pressure pain while motor disorder changes the loading that produces it. The point is not that neuropathy was impossible. It is that the record's later certainty must be answerable to evidence distinct from the hypothesis itself.

IV / SEVEN MOVEMENTS

The warning

The gravest cost of an unresolved explanation is not always diagnostic. Sometimes it is the life that must continue while the explanation remains unsettled.

By March 2023, the records no longer describe simply a man with difficult feet. Pain was overtaking his capacity to endure daily life. On 26 March, his GP wrote urgently to neurology. He reported that the pain was extreme at times and was 'causing death wish'. He wrote, 'Despite his facade, I don't think he is doing well,' and asked for review 'before we get into trouble here'. A Parkinson's nurse recorded debilitating pain despite pregabalin and the patient's description of having 'no quality of life'. These were not phrases found years later in a retrospective memoir. They were warnings circulating through the treating system while the suffering was occurring.

Think of the mismatch between the language available to the patient and the life it had to carry. A dose can be increased; a referral made; a letter answered. None of those actions necessarily restores an ordinary morning or gives someone confidence that tomorrow will be bearable. The GP's phrase 'despite his facade' is especially unsettling. It records a person who could still present himself to the world while the doctor who knew him feared that appearances concealed danger. Whatever anyone believed about the mechanism of foot pain, that danger required its own response.

The visible record does document neurological contact and treatment changes. It does not allow the reader to locate a distinct suicide-risk assessment, safety plan or coordinated safeguarding account answering the GP's warning. That is a bounded documentary observation, not a claim that nobody spoke to him or tried to help. But it creates a question that cannot be answered by debating whether neuropathy or dystonia better explains the feet: when a treating GP reports a death wish, how does a hospital know that the warning has reached someone responsible for the person's immediate safety? And where is that responsibility recorded so that the next clinician can see it?

Later in 2023, an observation entered the specialist record that brought the feet back into a distinctly neurological frame. The patient described severe foot pain, reduced mobility and shuffling, worse in the morning and when Sinemet had not yet taken effect. A movement-disorders clinician considered the syndrome suggestive of undertreated Parkinsonism and described the leg pain and shuffling as OFF-related. A Stalevo trial was considered reasonable.

Nothing in that assessment required all foot pain to be dystonic. OFF-related pain can have complex origins, and structural lesions can coexist with neurological fluctuation. Its significance lies elsewhere. It made the patient's medication state relevant to understanding the pain, rather than only to managing movement. It supplied another reason to reconstruct the relation among gait, toe posture, foot loading and symptoms that had originally travelled together in the GP's referral.

The treatment response mattered. The absence of a clear historical reassessment mattered too. One related to what could be done that month; the other to the story through which future doctors would understand the preceding years.

In March 2025, the patient described the first 150 steps of morning as torture. First he had to get out of bed. Then he had to put his feet down. Only then could he begin the count. A medical record can hold that sentence in a few lines. The body has to complete every one of those steps.

Then came a change in the timing and formulation of Sinemet, including controlled-release treatment intended to carry dopaminergic effect through the night. The patient reported extraordinary relief. His recollection of how he obtained and first took the medication differs in some respects from the neurologist's later account of the prescription chronology; both belong in the record. They need not be artificially reconciled to recognise that the improvement itself became a central fact in the subsequent correspondence.

But that valid caution did not settle the matter that the patient raised. Where was the evidence that the underlying pain had first been appropriately classified as neuropathic? What had happened to the original dystonic-looking lower limb and the early drug-withdrawal observations? Could altered neurological tone influence mechanical loading and thus the pain of real corns and deformities? It was possible to accept the limits of a drug-response inference without treating the response as incapable of reopening the earlier division.

V / SEVEN MOVEMENTS

The record answers back

On 19 March 2025, the patient sent a complaint titled Diagnostic Anchoring, Bias, and Failure to Reassess (2017–2025). It was received or logged by Quality and Patient Safety on 25 March. The difference between sending and logging is mundane, but in a story about documents it matters to keep even mundane chronology accurate. His complaint challenged not only prescriptions but the structure by which his feet had been understood. He asked for independent review, records, explanations and preservation of material. It was, in a formal register, the same question he remembered asking behind the curtain eight years earlier.

But the complaint did not contain the documentary explanation assembled later. Its author knew the years of pain, the treatment response, and the unsettling possibility that the clinical framing had prevented reassessment. He expressly said that he did not possess every internal note or referral. He asked the hospital for the records, an independent review and an explanation. The protest of July 2017 belonged to his lived history; the source-by-source reconstruction of its omission from the institutional account belonged to the months that followed. To place the finished reconstruction inside the March complaint would falsify the chronology of discovery.

The neurologist's response, dated 25 March, described a history of thoughtful management. It referred to risks of levodopa and dyskinesia, to pregabalin, to possible neuropathic pain, and to relief after Sinemet CR. It defended the early caution by returning explicitly to the 2017 treatment rationale. It also described neuropathic pain in a way that treated the category as available while leaving the underlying cause uncertain.

One cannot read intentions directly from defensive prose. Clinicians have every reason to explain difficult decisions and resist what they believe are mistaken accusations. But language can perform institutional work regardless of private motive. The response preserved a coherent history of clinical judgment. It did not visibly supply the independent evidential event that converted the original “perhaps” into a durable neuropathic classification. Nor did it visibly reconstruct the patient's pre-discharge objection to the adequacy of the foot investigation.

The institution's Quality and Patient Safety process treated the complaint as a matter of clinical judgment. That categorisation has a practical logic. A complaint about diagnostic anchoring, medicines and pain belongs partly to clinical expertise. Hospitals must seek accounts from clinicians who made decisions; a conscientious review could scarcely proceed without their testimony. But the category has limits. If the allegation is that the source record itself transformed a patient's words, lost a relational question and later became the authority against which the patient was judged, asking whether the original clinical judgments were defensible is not the whole investigation.

The patient was challenging two things at once: whether particular clinical decisions were reasonable, and whether the information on which those decisions rested had been preserved accurately enough to justify reliance upon it. The second question cannot be settled by asking the original decision-maker to restate the first.

An independent clinical reviewer would want to know whether the pain, dystonia, structural foot problems and medication responses were adequately integrated. A patient-safety investigator would begin earlier, at the handover between the GP referral and the hospital's shortened summary: which words were lost, who noticed, and what mechanism would have corrected the omission? An information-governance specialist would trace the handwritten note into the typed discharge account and subsequent letters. An ethicist would ask what it means when a patient protests before discharge but that objection has no place in the official narrative. Those are different inquiries, not variations of the same request for the neurologist's opinion. The hospital's communicated review outcome did not show the reader how each was undertaken; the full underlying assessment would be needed to answer that question.

The patient's formulation is stark: was his file kept in Neurology, or was it kept in Governance? Not literally which cabinet held the chart. He means whether the clinical story was chiefly being reopened in pursuit of better understanding, or managed so that its original disposition remained defensible. A record can function as a governance object long before anyone labels it as one. Clinical correspondence can protect a prior decision; a review can protect the correspondence; an institution can protect the review. At every stage the paperwork may be orderly.

VI / SEVEN MOVEMENTS

The investigator was the patient

What happened after the complaint cannot be told as though the patient already possessed the understanding it took him eighteen months to develop. He began with the experience of pain and an answer that did not satisfy him. The record reconstruction came later, by degrees, as he pursued documents and compared institutional accounts.

A complaint is not a completed investigation. On 19 March 2025, the patient knew that the foot pain had dominated his life, that medication appeared to alter it, and that the history of its explanation no longer made sense to him. He requested the underlying records because he did not yet know what they would reveal. This matters. The patient of March 2025 must not be credited with the archival understanding built during the following eighteen months. Nor should the exhaustion of that investigation be made to vanish behind the neat confidence of its conclusions.

On 15 April 2025, the first institutional answer arrived. Quality and Patient Safety told him that the complaint had been assessed as relating solely to clinical judgment and forwarded to the treating neurologist. The patient had asked why a disabling symptom had remained inadequately explained for eight years; the institution asked the author of the longstanding explanation to answer. This was not necessarily an improper clinical referral. It was, however, a poor substitute for independently tracing how the explanation had entered the record in the first place. The question about medical judgment was real. The question about the history of that judgment was different.

The correspondence shows his questions changing in real time. On 19 March he wrote that he did not have access to every internal note, referral or letter. On 12 June he was no longer requesting only an explanation of treatment: he asked for rectification and annotation of inaccurate or incomplete medical information. On 7 July he sought records of the bedside teaching encounter in which, by his account, he had objected to leaving hospital with his feet unresolved. He asked for notes, rosters and supervisory traces: not proof that the encounter never happened if nothing was found, but a way of learning whether the institution had preserved it. On 18 July he asked Quality and Patient Safety to explain what kind of review was underway, whether a meeting had taken place and how an advocate could help. Every new request reveals the previous unanswered question. He was trying to locate the means by which the hospital knew what it claimed to know.

On 5 August, the access decision said the hospital held no personal data responsive to several requested categories of student involvement, teaching and ward-round material, while supplying clinical notes separately. The absence of those categories did not settle his account of the protest. It gave him another boundary to understand: the difference between an event and an institution’s surviving record of it. In September, the hospital communicated the Serious Incident Management Team’s conclusion that care had shown no deficit. A separate decision declined to rectify historical medical records, reasoning that a clinical opinion had to remain accurately recorded as an opinion made at the time. That is a defensible principle of record preservation. But preservation is not the same as an independent investigation into whether an opinion had been enlarged, repeated or relied upon beyond its original evidence.

By January 2026, Quality and Patient Safety expressly maintained that a later dystonia diagnosis did not require reopening completed governance findings or amending historical records. Meanwhile, the patient continued pursuing records, regulatory questions and contradictions across the correspondence. The February genetic result added another piece, but even a confirmed PRKN disorder could not by itself reconstruct what the hospital had done with the presenting foot phenotype in 2017. That reconstruction required putting the sources beside one another, observing shifts in wording, and refusing to count repetition as independent confirmation.

Other correspondence, including exchanges with his GP, may show the isolation of this period more intimately, but no particular scene or sequence from those emails belongs here until their dates and words have been checked. The letters already in view describe a quieter ordeal. A man who had spent years learning the daily mechanics of walking was now learning another set of mechanics: complaints, access requests, rectification, incident reviews, regulatory replies. To discover where one sentence came from, he first had to discover which office might hold it. To challenge the authority of a diagnosis, he had to distinguish the historical existence of a medical opinion from the accuracy of the facts attributed to the patient. He had not entered hospital as an archivist. He became one because the answers were not being reconstructed for him.

Eight days in hospital. Nearly eight years to the complaint. Eighteen further months tracing the paper history. The sequence was not a single epiphany: March concerned the care he had lived; June, the accuracy of its description; July, the missing trace of his own objection; September, the meaning of institutional approval; January, the refusal to reopen earlier governance conclusions in light of later diagnosis. The evidence of the investigation is not that he knew the answer from the start. It is that the hospital’s replies repeatedly left him with further work to do.

To call the patient an institutional liability is to name a possibility rather than a fact about a hidden internal designation. He had a condition that could reveal an information-integrity problem, and he was the one person who remembered what had been said before the document was finished. His life supplied the continuing contradictions. If the clinical record was also performing a protective function, then his pain became the place where the institution's unresolved history was managed. The risk was not simply that the hospital might look wrong. The risk was that the person might go on suffering while the existing account remained intact.

The same patient remained under care, and clinicians saw his condition changing. Yet the available material does not show a comparable reconstruction of the original question about his feet. Eighteen months after the complaint, the person who had lived the clinical history was still carrying the labour of making its documentary history intelligible. The patient’s investigation was not a substitute for the hospital’s duty to examine a safety concern. It was evidence of how difficult it had become to obtain such an examination.

In February 2026, genetic confirmation finally supplied a biological answer to an important part of the 2017 differential. Testing identified biallelic pathogenic PRKN variants, and a specialist attributed both the patient's parkinsonism and dystonia to Parkin disease. This was not an entirely foreign diagnosis arriving from nowhere. PRKN had been entertained during the admission; Healy had regarded PARK2 as probable later that year. What had remained hypothetical in the genetic account was now confirmed.

The result cannot be used as a time machine. It does not automatically establish what treatment a reasonable doctor should have chosen in July 2017. It does not prove that the patient's corns were dystonic, that peripheral neuropathy never coexisted, or that all later pain would have vanished under an earlier prescription. Medicine is rarely that tidy. But the result does change the historical importance of the 2017 dystonic-looking lower limb. Dystonia was not an irrelevant descriptive flourish. It belonged to the disease ultimately confirmed.

There is a temptation to make the final test result the moral climax: the patient was right; the hospital was wrong. That would flatten the truth. The hospital recognised parkinsonism; it suspected PRKN; it treated genuine neurological disease. The deepest failure alleged here lies not in failing to anticipate a result, but in the apparent inability of an initial uncertain partition to remain genuinely corrigible as relevant evidence multiplied.

VII / SEVEN MOVEMENTS

Who was safety for?

In a hospital, authorship carries authority. A consultant's discharge letter is not treated as the equivalent of a patient's recollection scribbled in a notebook. It conveys expertise, responsibility and apparent completeness. The GP may read it as a summary of the hospital's work. A nurse practitioner may use it to understand the boundaries of current management. A complaints officer may ask its author to explain the reasoning it records. Each use is sensible in isolation. Together, they can turn the authored document into the most influential account of the life it only partly describes.

The record was installed as authority early. What accumulated afterward were collisions: medication responses, returning foot pain, reclassification of abnormal movement, specialist observations of OFF-related symptoms, intolerable suffering, patient complaints and eventual genetic confirmation. Some collisions changed treatment; none is shown in the accessible material to have decisively broken the founding partition. The question is therefore not how the record gradually became authoritative, but how an authoritative record survived repeated occasions on which its assumptions might have been revised.

Imagine the relevant papers spread across one table. The GP referral is on the left, with painful feet and clawing toes still attached to the abnormal gait. Beside it lies the shortened hospital summary. Then the handwritten account of two different sensations; the typed account of different problems; the discharge impression proposing possible neuropathy; later medication observations; the urgent GP letter of 2023; the patient's first complaint; the neurologist's defence; the governance decisions. At every junction a specialist can ask a precise question. The clinician asks what was examined. The records specialist asks what was transformed. The safety investigator asks what could have interrupted the sequence. The patient asks why the feet that governed his days never received an explanation of their relationship to the illness that governed his movements. No single answer substitutes for the others.

Patient safety is often narrated through the emergency that almost happened: the wrong drug caught before administration, the abnormal result noticed before discharge, the deteriorating patient rescued at the last hour. Here the missed opportunity had a quieter shape. On 10 July 2017, a man asked a specialist to look again at the part of the problem he believed remained unattended. If the event happened as he describes, the request supplied contemporaneous notice of a gap before the record was closed. It was an opportunity to correct this patient's course and perhaps to identify a risk in the compression of complex referrals.

Nothing requires us to imagine that one more examination would have solved everything. A detailed lower-limb assessment might still have produced a mixed explanation. Podiatry might still have been necessary. Levodopa might still have been delayed. The difference would have been epistemic: an openly stated uncertainty, an acknowledged patient objection, a documented owner of the unresolved relationship and a rule for when new evidence should trigger reconsideration. That would have changed what later clinicians were entitled to assume.

The hospital need not presume its own doctors wrong. It does need a way to test whether the source material behind their judgments is complete, whether a patient warning was handed to the right person, whether a clinician's later account depends upon earlier words he himself supplied, and whether new evidence changes the historical explanation rather than only the next prescription. Medical judgment, record integrity and institutional learning are different responsibilities. The case calls for all three.

The final image is not a medical chart but a man about to go home. He has been told he has a serious neurological condition. The staff have worked hard. The discharge is approaching. He looks down at his feet, still the part of the story he understands most immediately, and asks for them to be addressed before the journey begins.

Perhaps the most important safety intervention in that room would have been neither a drug nor a test. Perhaps it would have been to preserve the question intact: these feet remain part of the neurological problem until their relationship has been investigated or explicitly, responsibly left open. Such a sentence would not have cured him. It might have changed what the next eight years were allowed to mean.

A hospital must be able to write a patient's history. The harder obligation is to ensure that, when the patient and the evidence challenge that history, the history can still be rewritten.
